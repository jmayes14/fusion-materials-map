Material: lithium

Fusion needs lithium. Nobody can trace it yet.

Fusion power plants run on a fuel called tritium, which is almost impossible to find in nature. So the plants will have to make their own, using a special kind of lithium. This page follows that lithium from the ground to the power plant, and shows who could get hurt along the way.

How to read this page

Every fact links to where it came from. When people accuse a company of something, we say who made the claim. A trade record shows two companies did business, but not what was in the box. When we don't know something, we say so. Not knowing doesn't mean a company is clean or guilty.

Words to know

Lithium-6
A rare type of lithium. Only about 7 out of every 100 lithium atoms are this kind. Fusion plants need much more of it than that.
Tritium
The fuel fusion plants burn. Plants make it by hitting lithium-6 with tiny particles called neutrons.
Enrichment
Sorting lithium to collect more of the lithium-6 kind. Until now this has mostly been done to build nuclear weapons.
Isotope
A version of an element. Lithium-6 and lithium-7 are two isotopes of lithium.
Sanctions list
A government list of companies people in that country are not allowed to do business with.
UFLPA
A U.S. law that blocks imports made with forced labor in Xinjiang, a region of China.

    Part 2 · What should change

    Clean up fusion's lithium before the industry grows

    Our main idea: if taxpayer money pays for fusion, the companies should have to show where their lithium came from. Governments should also pay for cleaner ways to make lithium-6, so nobody has to buy it from weapons programs.

    The problem

    Fusion needs much less lithium than batteries do, but it needs the rare lithium-6 kind. One power plant needs about 100 tonnes of it. Right now, almost all the factories that can make lithium-6 belong to nuclear weapons programs in Russia and China, and they use toxic mercury to do it.

    The last time the U.S. did this, at Oak Ridge, Tennessee, hundreds of tonnes of mercury leaked into the ground and a creek. They are still cleaning it up. And before lithium even gets to that step, it can pass through Indigenous land in South America, mines in Zimbabwe that use child labor, and factories in China linked to forced labor.

    Why now

    Fusion power plants are about 10 years away from needing large amounts of lithium-6. It's much easier to set fair rules now, before companies sign long contracts and build factories. Much of the money in fusion today comes from governments, so governments have a say.

    Three recommendations

    1. Attach rules to government fusion money

    Any fusion project paid for by the U.S. Department of Energy, the U.K. fusion agency, or the international ITER project should report where its lithium was mined, processed, and sorted. It should follow the international guidelines for responsible mining. And mines on Indigenous land should show that local communities agreed.

    Who would do it: the U.S. Department of Energy, the U.K. Atomic Energy Authority, and ITER.

    2. Pay for clean lithium-6, and stop buying from weapons programs

    Fund new ways to make lithium-6 without mercury. Companies like Precision Periodic, Molten Salt Solutions, and Marathon Fusion are already working on this. Government-funded projects should not buy lithium-6 made with mercury or made inside weapons programs. That also matches a global treaty on mercury.

    Who would do it: the U.S. Department of Energy, Congress, and the U.K. energy department.

    3. Close a gap in import rules

    Make clear that the U.S. forced-labor import law covers lithium-6 and other special forms of lithium, and require importers to say where it came from. Shipping records show why this matters: a U.S. company buys from five Russian nuclear plants, and a Chinese company on the forced-labor list owns a lithium plant. Labs that buy from middlemen may never see where their lithium started.

    Who would do it: U.S. Customs and Border Protection, the Department of Homeland Security, and the Department of Commerce.

    The tech idea: a "Lithium Passport"

    Think of it like a passport for each batch of lithium. Every time the lithium changes hands, from the mine to the power plant, that step gets recorded, along with what was checked. Before buying, a fusion company would look up every company in the chain using tools like Sayari, the same way this project did by hand.

    The passport should also let affected communities file complaints, so it's not only companies telling the story.

    Downsides to be honest about

    • Rules cost money and could slow down early experiments. Small research amounts, under about 1 kilogram, could get a pass at first.
    • The passport only works if the factories take part. Government money is what gets them to.
    • Records show who traded with whom, not how workers are treated. Inspections and community voices are still needed.
    • Lithium-6 can be used in weapons, so some records would have to stay private.

    How we'd know it's working

    • More government-funded fusion lithium comes with a known mine.
    • More lithium-6 is made without mercury and outside weapons programs.
    • Communities file complaints and they get resolved.

    How we made this page

    This is the short version. The full explanation covers every step in detail.

    Finding facts. We used Tavily, an AI search tool, to find government reports, research papers, news stories, and company announcements. For important facts, we pulled up the full original page and checked the exact wording. Each source on this page is marked "via Tavily."

    Following companies. We used Sayari to look up companies by their official registration numbers, see who they ship to and buy from, and see who owns them. We didn't trust searches by company name alone, because they mixed up different companies. Each source from there is marked "via Sayari."

    Our rules. We don't call any company "good" or "bad." When someone makes an accusation, we say who made it. When a shipping record doesn't show what was shipped, we say so. Guesses about the future are labeled as estimates.

    Use of AI. This page was built and written with help from Claude, an AI assistant. Every fact links to a real source.
