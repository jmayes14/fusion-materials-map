1. The research question

Where will the lithium for fusion power plants come from, who could get hurt along the way, and what could make it safer and fairer?

This is a class project called "Mapping the Human Cost of Fusion Energy Materials." The assignment asked for a map of the supply chain, a website, and a short plan to fix the problems found.

This first version looks only at lithium. Other fusion materials, like beryllium and tungsten, could come later.

2. Why lithium

Fusion power plants run on a fuel called tritium, which barely exists in nature. Plants plan to make it from a rare kind of lithium called lithium-6. Only about 7 of every 100 lithium atoms are lithium-6, so it has to be sorted out. That sorting has mostly been done to build nuclear weapons. Lithium mining also already has known problems with water, land, and labor. That made lithium the most important material to look at first.

3. The six steps of the supply chain

The journey is split into six steps. Each step is where the lithium changes form or changes hands, and where different people could be affected.

StepWhat happensWho it affects
1. DigLithium is pumped from salt flats or mined from rockNearby communities, Indigenous peoples, mine workers
2. ProcessIt's turned into clean lithium chemicals, mostly in ChinaFactory workers, including possible forced labor
3. SortThe rare lithium-6 is collected, often using mercuryNeighbors of these plants, and everyone if it helps build weapons
4. Make partsLithium-6 is made into fuel-making partsFactory workers handling toxic materials
5. UseThe parts go into fusion machinesPlant workers; this is where government money decides things
6. AfterwardUsed parts are recycled or thrown away as wasteCommunities near waste sites

4. Finding facts (Tavily)

Tavily is an AI search engine. It was used in four steps:

  1. Search. About 20 searches covered topics like "who makes lithium-6 today," "mercury pollution at Oak Ridge," "Indigenous water rights in Chile," and "child labor in Zimbabwe's lithium."
  2. Pick trustworthy sources. Government reports, research papers, and well-known news outlets and advocacy groups came first. A company's own announcement is labeled "Company's own claim."
  3. Check the exact wording. For important facts, the full original page was pulled up to make sure each sentence on this site matches what it says, including the numbers.
  4. Cut what can't be confirmed. If a source couldn't be opened or a detail couldn't be proven, it was taken out.

27 of the 35 sources came from Tavily. Each one is marked "via Tavily."

5. Following companies (Sayari)

Sayari is a database of company registrations, shipping records, government contracts, and sanctions lists. The web tells you what's been reported. Sayari shows which real companies actually do business together.

  1. Confirm the right company. Many companies have similar names, so each one was matched by its official registration numbers, not just its name.
  2. Look at who they trade with. The records show who each company ships to, who it buys from, who owns it, and who hires it.
  3. Narrow by product. Shipping records use codes for different kinds of goods. Results were filtered to the codes for isotopes and lithium chemicals, so unrelated goods like batteries weren't counted.
  4. Avoid bad searches. Searching shipping records by company name alone gave wrong results, so those searches weren't used.

8 of the 35 sources came from Sayari. Each one is marked "via Sayari." Opening those links requires a Sayari account.

6. Rules for fairness

  • No "good" or "bad" labels for companies. The site reports what the sources show.
  • Every accusation names who made it. These are marked "People's claims, reported in the news."
  • A trade isn't proof of what was shipped. When the records don't show what was in the box, the site says "contents unknown."
  • Guesses about the future are marked "Estimate."
  • Gaps are shown. Every step lists open questions. Not knowing doesn't mean a company is clean or guilty.
  • Only official lists are used. Government sanctions and forced-labor lists are cited, but not Sayari's own risk scores.

7. How harm was judged

Each step was checked against three questions: who could get hurt, how, and who reported it. The harms fall into five kinds: land and water, child labor, forced labor, pollution, and weapons and sanctions.

Each harm on the site says who reported it:

  • Government: a government list, record, or decision.
  • Journalists and advocacy groups: news stories or reports from human rights organizations.
  • Comes with the work: a known danger of the job itself, like handling toxic metals, even without a specific reported case.

8. How the maps were made

  • The story (home page) walks through 8 stops. At each stop the map fits that stop's places into the space beside the card, highlights them, and draws arrows for real shipments from company records.
  • The interactive map shows all 23 places, as a flat world map or a globe. Each dot shows its step number. Readers can pick one step at a time or take the tour.
  • Dots sit at the city or site. Bigger, lighter dots mark a whole country or region. One dot (Isoflex USA) marks its state, because its exact address isn't confirmed.
  • Arrows appear only where a source shows a connection. They point from seller to buyer. Solid lines are public deals, and dashed lines are shipping records.
  • The country outlines come from a free public map dataset (Natural Earth, through the world-atlas package).

9. Where the solutions come from

Each idea in the plan comes straight from a finding:

  • Rules on government money: most fusion projects today are paid for by governments, so that money is the easiest way to require better sourcing.
  • Clean lithium-6: the mercury pollution at Oak Ridge shows the old method is dangerous, and new companies show cleaner methods are possible.
  • Import rules: shipping records show a U.S. company buying from Russian nuclear plants, and a Chinese company on the forced-labor list owning a lithium plant.
  • The Lithium Passport: it takes the checks done by hand for this project, looking up every company in the chain, and makes them routine for every batch.

10. Use of AI

  • Claude, an AI assistant, helped build the website, run the searches, and write the first drafts.
  • Every fact links to a real source, and important facts were checked against the original. Anything the AI wrote that couldn't be backed up was taken out.

11. What is still unknown

The chain doesn't reach a fusion company yet. No fusion company has said where its lithium comes from, and the biggest one doesn't have a lithium supplier in shipping records yet.

Shipping records don't show everything. Isoflex USA buys isotopes or lithium chemicals from Russian nuclear plants, but the records don't say exactly which ones, or whether any went to a fusion lab.

Some records are incomplete. The owner of the Russian plant NCCP isn't listed. Zimbabwe's lithium can't be followed past a trading company in China.

Some countries keep secrets. Russia, China, and North Korea don't publish how much lithium-6 they make.

The search had limits. It was done on one day, 9 November 2026, and couldn't find everything that exists.

12. How to check this work

  • Every fact on the site links to its source.
  • All the notes, sources, and company records are in one file: research notes.
  • The website's code is public on GitHub, including every change.
