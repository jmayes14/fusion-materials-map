1. What we wanted to know

Our question: Where will the lithium for fusion power plants come from, who could get hurt along the way, and what could make it safer and fairer?

This is for a class project called "Mapping the Human Cost of Fusion Energy Materials." We had to make a map of the supply chain, a website, and a short plan to fix the problems we found.

This first version looks only at lithium. Other fusion materials, like beryllium and tungsten, could come later.

2. Why we picked lithium

Fusion power plants run on a fuel called tritium, which barely exists in nature. Plants plan to make it from a rare kind of lithium called lithium-6. Only about 7 of every 100 lithium atoms are lithium-6, so it has to be sorted out. That sorting has mostly been done to build nuclear weapons. Lithium mining also already has known problems with water, land, and labor. That made lithium the most important material to look at first.

3. How we split up the supply chain

We broke the journey into six steps. Each step is where the lithium changes form or changes hands, and where different people could be affected.

StepWhat happensWho it affects
1. DigLithium is pumped from salt flats or mined from rockNearby communities, Indigenous peoples, mine workers
2. ProcessIt's turned into clean lithium chemicals, mostly in ChinaFactory workers, including possible forced labor
3. SortThe rare lithium-6 is collected, often using mercuryNeighbors of these plants, and everyone if it helps build weapons
4. Make partsLithium-6 is made into fuel-making partsFactory workers handling toxic materials
5. UseThe parts go into fusion machinesPlant workers; this is where government money decides things
6. AfterwardUsed parts are recycled or thrown away as wasteCommunities near waste sites

4. How we found facts (Tavily)

Tavily is an AI search engine. Here's how we used it:

  1. We searched. We ran about 20 searches on topics like "who makes lithium-6 today," "mercury pollution at Oak Ridge," "Indigenous water rights in Chile," and "child labor in Zimbabwe's lithium."
  2. We picked trustworthy sources. We preferred government reports, research papers, and well-known news outlets and advocacy groups. When we used a company's own announcement, we labeled it "Company's own claim."
  3. We checked the exact wording. For important facts, we pulled up the full original page and made sure our sentence matched what it said, including the numbers.
  4. We cut what we couldn't confirm. If we couldn't open a source or prove a detail, we took it out.

27 of our 35 sources came from Tavily. Each one is marked "via Tavily" on the main page.

5. How we followed companies (Sayari)

Sayari is a database of company registrations, shipping records, government contracts, and sanctions lists. The web tells you what's been reported. Sayari shows which real companies actually do business together.

  1. We made sure we had the right company. Many companies have similar names, so we matched them by official registration numbers, not just names.
  2. We looked at who they trade with. We checked who each company ships to, who it buys from, who owns it, and who hires it.
  3. We narrowed by product. Shipping records use codes for different kinds of goods. We filtered for the codes for isotopes and lithium chemicals, so we weren't counting unrelated things like batteries.
  4. We avoided bad searches. Searching shipping records by company name alone gave wrong results, so we didn't use them.

8 of our 35 sources came from Sayari. Each one is marked "via Sayari." You need a Sayari account to open those links.

6. Our rules for being fair

  • No labels like "good" or "bad" companies. We just report what the sources show.
  • We say who made each accusation. These are marked "People's claims, reported in the news."
  • A trade isn't proof of what was shipped. We say "contents unknown" when the records don't show what was in the box.
  • Guesses about the future are marked "Estimate."
  • We show what we don't know. Every step lists open questions. Not knowing doesn't mean a company is clean or guilty.
  • We only use official lists. We mention government sanctions and forced-labor lists, but not Sayari's own risk scores.

7. How we judged harm

For each step, we asked three questions: who could get hurt, how, and who reported it. We grouped the harms into five kinds: land and water, child labor, forced labor, pollution, and weapons and sanctions.

Each harm on the main page says who reported it:

  • Government: a government list, record, or decision.
  • Journalists and advocacy groups: news stories or reports from human rights organizations.
  • Comes with the work: a known danger of the job itself, like handling toxic metals, even without a specific reported case.

8. How we made the globe

  • The globe shows 23 places, colored to match the six steps.
  • Dots are placed at the city or site. Bigger, lighter dots mark a whole country or region. One dot (Isoflex USA) marks its state, because we don't have its exact address.
  • We only drew a line between two places when a source showed a connection. Solid lines are public deals. Dashed lines are shipping records.
  • The world map outline comes from a free public map dataset.

9. How we came up with solutions

Each idea in our plan comes straight from something we found:

  • Rules on government money: most fusion projects today are paid for by governments, so that money is the easiest way to require better sourcing.
  • Clean lithium-6: the mercury pollution at Oak Ridge shows the old method is dangerous, and new companies show cleaner methods are possible.
  • Import rules: shipping records show a U.S. company buying from Russian nuclear plants, and a Chinese company on the forced-labor list owning a lithium plant.
  • The Lithium Passport: it takes what this project did by hand, checking every company in the chain, and makes it routine for every batch.

10. How we used AI

  • Claude, an AI assistant, helped build the website, run the searches, and write the first drafts.
  • Every fact links to a real source, and important facts were checked against the original. When the AI wrote something we couldn't back up, we took it out.
  • The student chose the topic, guided the research, checked the sources, and stands behind the conclusions.

11. What we couldn't figure out

We couldn't connect the chain to a fusion company. No fusion company has said where its lithium comes from, and the biggest one doesn't have a lithium supplier in shipping records yet.

Shipping records don't show everything. We know Isoflex USA buys isotopes or lithium chemicals from Russian nuclear plants, but not exactly which ones, or whether any went to a fusion lab.

Some records are incomplete. We couldn't find who owns the Russian plant NCCP. We lost track of Zimbabwe's lithium in China.

Some countries keep secrets. Russia, China, and North Korea don't publish how much lithium-6 they make.

Our search had limits. We searched on one day, 9 November 2026, and couldn't find everything that exists.

12. How to check our work

  • Every fact on the main page links to its source.
  • All our notes, sources, and company records are in one file: research notes.
