Why lithium?

Why fusion needs lithium, and how it becomes lithium-6

Before following lithium around the world, it helps to know why fusion needs it at all, how it gets out of the ground, and how ordinary lithium is turned into the rare lithium-6 that fusion plants need.

1 · The fuel problem

Why fusion needs lithium

Fusion power plants plan to run on two kinds of hydrogen: deuterium, which is easy to get from seawater, and tritium, which is not.

There is very little tritium on Earth. Right now, all the tritium you can buy is a leftover from one type of nuclear power plant, called CANDU. Those plants are getting old, and the supply is expected to shrink after 2050.

So fusion plants will have to make their own tritium. They'll wrap the fusion reaction in a layer that contains lithium. When a tiny particle called a neutron flies out and hits a lithium atom, the atom splits into tritium and helium.

That makes lithium more than a building material. It's part of the fuel.

2 · Two kinds of lithium

Why it has to be lithium-6

Lithium comes in two natural kinds, called isotopes: lithium-6 and lithium-7. In a battery they act the same. The only difference is a tiny bit of weight inside the atom.

For making fuel, they behave very differently. Lithium-6 makes tritium easily and gives off heat doing it. Lithium-7 only makes tritium when hit by very fast neutrons, and it uses up energy to do so.

The problem is how little lithium-6 nature gives us. A few designs can use natural lithium, but most can't.

3 · Extraction

How lithium comes out of the ground

Lithium is common but spread very thin. On average, Earth's crust is only about 0.007% lithium, so it's taken from the few places where it's concentrated, in one of three ways.

6–18 months to dry
Chile and Argentina

Salty water from salt flats

  1. Wells pump salty underground water, called brine, from beneath salt flats. In Chile's Atacama Desert, each liter holds about 1.8 grams of lithium.
  2. The brine is spread into huge shallow ponds, and the desert sun and wind dry it out.
  3. What's left is a yellowish liquid that's about 6% lithium.

Why it matters: it takes water from one of the driest places on Earth, often on Indigenous land. Who gets hurt →

1,050 °C roasting
Australia and Zimbabwe

Mining rock

  1. Miners dig up rock containing a lithium mineral called spodumene, then crush it and separate out the waste.
  2. The powder is roasted at about 1,050 °C (1,920 °F), mixed with strong sulfuric acid, and roasted again.
  3. The lithium can now dissolve in water and be pulled out. It takes a lot of energy and chemicals.

Why it matters: open-pit mines bring dust, waste, and in some places child labor. The Dig step →

70–90% recovered
New and still growing

Pulling lithium straight from brine

  1. Brine is pumped up the same way, but it flows through special materials that grab only the lithium.
  2. No giant ponds are needed, and the leftover water can often go back underground.
  3. Ponds recover about 40 to 60% of the lithium. This could recover 70 to 90%.

Why it matters: less water lost and less land used, but it isn't widely used yet.

4 · Processing and sorting

How raw lithium becomes lithium-6

  1. Step A

    Clean it up

    Raw lithium is purified into lithium chemicals, the same ones batteries use. China does about 70% of this work.

  2. Step B

    Sort the atoms

    Lithium-6 is separated from lithium-7. The two are almost identical, so this is very hard. It's called enrichment.

  3. Step C

    Make fuel parts

    The lithium-6 is made into tiny ceramic beads, a liquid metal, or a melted salt, and packed around the fusion reaction. More →

The old way: sorting with mercury

The method used at large scale is called COLEX:

  1. Lithium is mixed into liquid mercury, a toxic metal.
  2. The mercury flows down tall columns while lithium dissolved in water flows up past it.
  3. Lithium-6 "prefers" the mercury, and lithium-7 prefers the water, so each pass sorts them a little more.
  4. After many passes, lithium-6 is taken back out of the mercury.

The U.S. did this at Oak Ridge, Tennessee, in the 1950s and 1960s, and hundreds of tonnes of mercury leaked into the land and a creek. Today only Russia and China still do it.

New ways, without mercury

Bead filters

Lithium flows through columns of tiny beads that sort the two kinds as they pass. Precision Periodic has U.S. government funding for this.

Liquid-to-liquid sorting

Two liquids that don't mix trade lithium back and forth, sorting a bit more each time. Molten Salt Solutions says it has deals to supply fusion companies.

Spinning it very fast

A machine spins electrically charged lithium gas to sort the two kinds by weight. Marathon Fusion says it has tested this.

None of these works at full factory scale yet. That's the gap our plan says governments should help close.
