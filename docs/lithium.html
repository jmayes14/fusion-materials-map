Why lithium?

Why fusion needs lithium, and how it becomes lithium-6

Before following lithium around the world, it helps to know why fusion needs it at all, how it gets out of the ground, and how ordinary lithium is turned into the rare lithium-6 that fusion plants need.

1 · The fuel problem

Why fusion needs lithium

Fusion power plants plan to run on two kinds of hydrogen: deuterium, which is easy to get from seawater, and tritium, which is not.

There is very little tritium on Earth. Right now, all the tritium you can buy is a leftover from one type of nuclear power plant, called CANDU. Those plants are getting old, and experts expect the supply to shrink after 2050.

So fusion plants will have to make their own tritium. They'll do it by wrapping the fusion reaction in a layer that contains lithium. When a tiny particle called a neutron flies out of the fusion reaction and hits a lithium atom, the lithium atom splits into tritium and helium.

That makes lithium more than a building material. It's part of the fuel.

2 · Two kinds of lithium

Why it has to be lithium-6

Lithium comes in two natural kinds, called isotopes: lithium-6 and lithium-7. They look and act the same in a battery. The difference is a tiny bit of weight inside the atom.

In nature, 92.5% of lithium is lithium-7. Only 7.5% is lithium-6.

For making fuel, they behave very differently. Lithium-6 makes tritium easily, and it gives off heat while doing it. Lithium-7 only makes tritium when hit by very fast neutrons, and it uses up energy to do so.

That's why most fusion designs want lithium that is 30 to 90% lithium-6, far more than nature provides. A few designs can use natural lithium, but most can't.

3 · Extraction

How lithium comes out of the ground

Lithium is common but spread very thin. On average, Earth's crust is only about 0.007% lithium. So it's taken from the few places where it's concentrated, in one of three ways.

Most of South America

1. Salty water from salt flats

  1. Wells pump salty underground water, called brine, up from beneath salt flats. In Chile's Atacama Desert, each liter holds about 1.8 grams of lithium.
  2. The brine is spread into huge shallow ponds. The desert sun and wind dry it out.
  3. After 6 to 18 months, what's left is a yellowish liquid that's about 6% lithium.

Why it matters: it takes water from some of the driest places on Earth, often on Indigenous land. See who gets hurt →

Australia, Zimbabwe

2. Mining rock

  1. Miners dig up rock containing a lithium mineral called spodumene. It's crushed and separated from the waste rock.
  2. The rock powder is roasted at about 1,050 °C (1,920 °F), then mixed with strong sulfuric acid and roasted again.
  3. That turns the lithium into a form that dissolves in water, so it can be pulled out. The process uses a lot of energy and chemicals.

Why it matters: open-pit mines bring dust, waste, and, in some places, child labor. See the Dig step →

New and still growing

3. Pulling lithium straight from brine

  1. Brine is pumped up the same way, but instead of drying in ponds, it flows through special materials that grab only the lithium.
  2. It doesn't need the giant ponds, and the leftover water can often be pumped back underground.
  3. Ponds recover about 40 to 60% of the lithium. This method could recover 70 to 90%.

Why it matters: it could mean less water lost and less land used, but it isn't widely used yet.

4 · Processing and sorting

How raw lithium becomes lithium-6

  1. A

    Clean it up

    The raw lithium from brine or rock is purified into lithium chemicals, like lithium carbonate or lithium hydroxide. These are what batteries use too. China does about 70% of this work.

  2. B

    Sort the atoms

    Next, lithium-6 has to be separated from lithium-7. Because the two are almost identical, this is very hard. It's called enrichment, the same word used for uranium.

  3. C

    Make fuel parts

    The lithium-6-rich material is turned into tiny ceramic beads, a liquid metal, or a melted salt, then packed around the fusion reaction to make tritium. See the Make parts step →

The old way: sorting with mercury

The method used at large scale, called COLEX, works like this :

  1. Lithium is mixed into liquid mercury, a toxic metal.
  2. That mercury mix flows down tall columns, while lithium dissolved in water flows up past it.
  3. Lithium-6 "prefers" the mercury, and lithium-7 prefers the water. So each pass leaves the mercury a little richer in lithium-6.
  4. After many passes, the lithium-6 is taken back out of the mercury.

The U.S. did this in Oak Ridge, Tennessee, in the 1950s and 1960s, and hundreds of tonnes of mercury leaked into the land and a creek. Today only Russia and China still do it.

New ways without mercury

Filters with special beads

Lithium flows through columns of tiny beads that sort the two kinds as they pass. Precision Periodic has U.S. government funding for this.

Liquid-to-liquid sorting

Two liquids that don't mix trade lithium back and forth, sorting a bit more each time. Molten Salt Solutions says it has deals to supply fusion companies.

Spinning it very fast

A machine spins electrically charged lithium gas very fast to sort the two kinds by weight. Marathon Fusion says it has tested this.

None of these is working at full factory scale yet. That's the gap our plan says governments should help close.

5 · Putting numbers on it

How much lithium one fusion plant needs

÷ 7.5% (the share of lithium-6 in nature)
1,300+ tonnesof ordinary lithium, at the very least, even if not a single lithium-6 atom were lost
÷ about 1.8 kg of lithium per cubic meter of Atacama brine
740,000+ m³of brine pumped from under the desert, roughly 300 Olympic swimming pools, before counting the lithium that ponds fail to recover

This is our own rough math using the sourced numbers above. Real amounts would be higher, because no sorting method is perfect and ponds recover only about half the lithium.
